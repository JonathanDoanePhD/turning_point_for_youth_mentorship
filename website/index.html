When does adult support matter most?

When, how, and for whom do supportive relationships shape young people's educational trajectories? Observational analyses of six U.S. cohorts compare associations of adult support measured at different ages (6 to 17), and of natural mentors who first became important at different ages. Each followed an analysis plan frozen in a public repository before its data were opened, which is not the same as an external preregistration.

TL;DR

The short version

  • The question: is support from adults (parents, teachers, mentors) more valuable at some ages than others for how far young people go in school?
  • What we did: nine analyses of six national U.S. studies following children and teens into adulthood. Each analysis plan was locked in before the data were opened.
  • What we found: support was modestly linked to more education, but no age stood out. Comparing brothers and sisters in the same family, praise at ages 6–9 and at 10–14 carried about the same weight.
  • For mentors: mentors who became important before age 14 showed no meaningful advantage over those who came along at 14–17. Whether relationships that last past 18 matter more is still unclear.
  • The fine print: these are associations in survey data, not proof of cause and effect, and moderate age differences cannot be ruled out. None of the studies tested a formal mentoring program.
  • So what: the evidence does not support waiting for, or rushing to, one "right age". The quality and continuity of relationships look like better bets.

Latest updates

  • Nov 2026Siblings compared: within families, praise at 6–9 and at 10–14 carried the same weight (equivalent within ±3 points). The apparent advantage of later praise came from differences between families (working paper v4).
  • Nov 2026Mentor continuity (Add Health, author-run): relationships that lasted to 18 leaned toward more college completion, but the result is inconclusive.
  • Nov 2026Audit accepted: a second analyst confirmed the results, and an independent R reproduction matched every survey estimate. Working paper v3.1 is approved.
  • Nov 2026When a mentor arrives: in Add Health, mentors who first became important before 14 were not associated with more college completion than those from 14–17 (run by the author outside AI tools).
  • Nov 2026Two replications with children's own reports (PSID, Future of Families): timing contrasts were inconclusive, with estimates for mothers leaning toward the earlier age.
  • Nov 2026Mentor-timing study read in full: mentors from before and during high school showed no difference; the "after high school" association is small and likely reflects continued education.
  • Nov 2026Independent review: a methodological audit found the results reproducible after a seeding fix and led to more cautious conclusions (audit report in Publications).
  • Nov 2026New full texts: the largest mentoring meta-analysis finds benefits at every age from under 8 to 18; older adolescents' matches end sooner.
  • Nov 2026Working paper v3 (revised after the independent review) brings all four analyses together with corrected conclusions.
  • Nov 2026Ages 6–9 vs 10–14 (NLSY79 Children and Young Adults): estimates did not favor middle childhood; the difference is imprecise.
  • Nov 2026Grade 9 vs grade 11 (HSLS:09): grade-9 math-teacher ratings were not more strongly associated with a BA than grade-11 ratings.
  • Nov 2026Timing vs duration (NLSY97): repeated reports of support go with more schooling, but cumulative, stability and loss-of-support explanations cannot be separated.
  • Nov 2026Full-text check of mentoring meta-analyses: no significant age moderation in between-study comparisons.
What the evidence shows so far. Reported support from parents (and, in grade 11, math teachers) was modestly associated with later education. No comparison across ages, grades or mentor start ages was statistically significant, and the direction was not consistent: some estimates favored the earlier age and some the later. For natural mentors, starting before 14 showed no meaningful advantage over starting at 14–17. Comparing siblings within the same families, praise at ages 6–9 and 10–14 carried the same weight. These data cannot show that timing does not matter. They are observational associations, not causal effects, and none concerns formal mentoring programs. Charts labeled illustrative contain no data.

Methods

Why timing is hard to measure

Suppose adult support helps every young person by the same amount at every age. Can a survey still make it look as if support matters more at some ages? Yes, if who receives support changes with age. Adjust the sliders to see how.

Illustrative — no data. This is a two-equation textbook model of selection and measurement error, not an estimate from any study.

The model behind the sliders

Outcome Y = β·S + γ·U + ε; support S = α(a)·U + ν, with U, ν, ε independent standard normal and α(a) linear in age between the two slider values. The observed support measure is S* = S + m with reliability r. The slope of Y on S* is r·(β + γ·α(a)/(α(a)² + 1)). Even with a constant β, the slope varies with age whenever α(a) does. Adjusting for good measures of U removes the bias; that is why the analysis plan adjusts for pre-exposure engagement and achievement, and reports sensitivity analyses for what remains unmeasured.

About the researcher

Jonathan Doane, Ph.D.

Jonathan Doane is a mathematician (Ph.D., universal algebra), applied AI researcher, data scientist and educational leader. His work spans machine learning, predictive modeling, NLP, statistical analysis and AI evaluation, alongside AI curriculum development, instructor leadership and evaluation of educational programs.

He began his own mathematics education in remedial algebra and went on to a doctorate. Teachers and mentors changed what he believed he could do. That experience motivates this project. It is not evidence for it. The project is designed so that its conclusions do not depend on that story.

The researcher hopes to found an evidence-based youth mentoring organization.
