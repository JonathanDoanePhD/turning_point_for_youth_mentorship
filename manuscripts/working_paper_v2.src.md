# When does adult support matter most? Evidence from three U.S. cohorts on the timing of supportive relationships and educational attainment

**Jonathan Doane, Ph.D.** · Working paper v2, 12 November 2026 · Not peer reviewed

## Abstract

**Background.** Programmes for young people are often justified by appeals to developmental windows:
periods, such as middle childhood or the first year of high school, when supportive adults are thought to
matter most. Evidence that the long-run payoff of support differs by age is scarce.
**Methods.** We ran four pre-planned analyses, each frozen before its data were opened, in three national
U.S. cohorts. (1) NLSY97: adolescents' reports of a very supportive mother or father, asked identically at ages
12–16 in 1997, related to highest grade completed around age 40. (2) HSLS:09: perceived math-teacher support at
the start of grade 9 versus grade 11, related to bachelor's-degree attainment by 2021. (3) NLSY79 Children and
Young Adults: mothers' repeated reports of praise and closeness at ages 6–9 versus 10–14, related to bachelor's
attainment. (4) NLSY97, exploratory: accumulation versus timing across four annual reports.
**Results.** Support was consistently, if modestly, associated with later attainment: about half a year of schooling
for a very supportive parent (NLSY97), and 1–2 percentage points of bachelor's-degree probability per SD of support
in early adolescence (CYA). No study found reliable evidence that support measured *earlier* carried more
weight. The only estimate pointing that way (fathers in NLSY97) was statistically equivalent to no difference. Age 16 vs 12: +0.26 years for mothers (−0.21 to 0.74), −0.18 for fathers (−0.64 to 0.27;
equivalent within ±0.2 SD). Grade 11 vs grade 9: +1.3 points (−0.4 to 3.1; equivalent within ±3 points). Ages 6–9
vs 10–14: −1.7 points (−3.9 to 0.6), favouring early adolescence. Each additional year of maternal support in
1997–2000 was associated with 0.22 more grades completed, and equal weighting of years was not rejected.
**Conclusions.** Across cohorts, adults, measures and age ranges, these data do not support a narrow early window,
including the freshman year, for supportive relationships. They are more consistent with support that matters
across childhood and adolescence and that accumulates. Associations are observational and could partly reflect
selection.

## 1. Introduction

The idea of a *turning point*, an experience that redirects a life trajectory, is central to life-course
research [@elder1998; @rutter1996]. Rutter stressed that turning-point effects are heterogeneous and depend
on the qualities of experience more than on age alone [@rutter1996]. Adolescence has been described as a period
of heightened plasticity and opportunity for intervention [@dahl2018], but a review of sensitive periods in
adolescent development found concrete evidence mostly lacking [@fuhrmann2015].

The clearest evidence on the timing of environmental exposure comes from housing mobility. Moving to a
lower-poverty neighbourhood before age 13 improved college attendance and earnings in the Moving to Opportunity
experiment, while moving as an adolescent did not [@chetty2016]. Quasi-experimental estimates suggest roughly
linear gains per childhood year of exposure [@chetty2018]. Skill-formation models emphasise that early skills
raise returns to later investment [@cunha2007], while later investment can substitute for earlier investment,
particularly for noncognitive skills [@cunha2010].

For supportive relationships the evidence on timing is thinner. Mentoring programmes show small-to-modest average
benefits [@dubois2002; @raposa2019], with the most benefit in relationships lasting a year or longer
[@grossman2002]. Teacher–student relationship quality is associated with engagement and achievement, more strongly
in higher grades [@roorda2011]. The transition to high school is a documented point of disruption [@benner2009],
and ninth-grade outcomes predict dropout [@neild2008]. The one study we found on when natural mentors became
important found the strongest association for mentors who became important after high school [@fruiht2013].

Reading the full texts of the meta-analyses that tested age gives the same picture: age has not reliably moderated
mentoring effects. In DuBois et al. [@dubois2002], effects for late childhood/early adolescence and middle/late
adolescence did not differ significantly (d = .17 vs .13). Raposa et al. [@raposa2019] found no significant association
between mean sample age (range about 9–16) and effect size, and called this somewhat surprising. Meta-analyses of natural
and youth-initiated mentoring found no significant age differences either [@vandam2018; @vandam2021]. Within one
school-based mentoring trial, impacts did not differ reliably between elementary and secondary students [@herrera2011], as reported in the publicly released trial report,. A meta-analysis of teacher–student relationships found one association
stronger in secondary than primary school, with the other four equal [@roorda2017]. Between-study age comparisons are
confounded with programme type, and none compares early childhood with adolescence. Individual-level data with
comparable measures at different ages are therefore needed.

We ask whether the long-run association of the *same* measure of adult support differs by when it was measured,
across three cohorts that together span ages 6 to 17. We keep four questions apart: whether a stage is especially
responsive to an input (developmental sensitivity); whether a transition has unusually large consequences
(decision significance); whether intervening is especially practical at a stage (intervention opportunity); and
whether an individual's trajectory visibly changes. Our designs address an observational version of the first.

## 2. General methods

Each analysis followed a written plan whose files were hashed and committed to a public repository before the
respondent-level data were opened. The plans were not registered with a public registry. Variable identifiers were
verified in official codebooks, and extracts were checked against codebook frequencies. Missing outcome and covariate
values were multiply imputed by chained equations with predictive mean matching [@white2011] and pooled by Rubin's
rules [@rubin1987]. Each primary contrast was tested both for difference from zero and for equivalence within a
pre-set smallest effect size of interest. Unmeasured confounding was summarised with E-values where relevant
[@vanderweele2017]. Reporting follows STROBE where applicable [@vonelm2007].

## 3. Study 1: parental support at ages 12–16 (NLSY97)

**Data and measures.** The NLSY97 is a nationally representative panel of 8,984 youths born 1980–84. In 1997,
youths living with a mother (father) figure were asked, "When you think about how she [he] acts toward you, in
general, would you say she [he] is very supportive, somewhat supportive, or not very supportive?" Exposure: very
supportive = 1. Moderator: age on 31 December 1996 (12–16). Outcome: highest grade completed, counted as observed
only if last interviewed in 2010 or later (age ≥ 25). Covariates (1997): log income-to-poverty ratio, household
structure, residential parents' education, sex, race/ethnicity, urban residence.

**Analysis.** Survey-weighted linear regression with a support × age interaction; Taylor-linearised variance with
the public strata and PSUs (117 design degrees of freedom); m = 40. Primary contrast Δ = θ(16) − θ(12), Holm-adjusted
across mothers and fathers; equivalence bound ±0.20 SD. A paired-PSU jackknife reproduced the standard errors to three
decimals.

**Results.** For 8,607 youths (mother item) and 6,436 (father item), a very supportive report was associated with
0.47 more years of schooling at age 14 (mother: 95% CI 0.31–0.63; father: 0.29–0.64), roughly 0.15 SD. Mothers: θ(12)
= 0.34, θ(16) = 0.60, Δ = +0.26 (−0.21 to 0.74); equivalence inconclusive. Fathers: θ(12) = 0.56, θ(16) = 0.38,
Δ = −0.18 (−0.64 to 0.27); equivalent within ±0.2 SD. Holm-adjusted p = .54 for both. Estimating each age separately
gave a non-monotone pattern. Adding a cognitive test score reduced θ(14) by a fifth to a quarter, and the E-value for θ(14)
was 1.56.

## 4. Study 2: the freshman year — math-teacher support in grade 9 vs grade 11 (HSLS:09)

**Data and measures.** HSLS:09 sampled 9th graders in fall 2009. Students rated their current math teacher on three
items repeated with identical wording in spring 2012 (grade 11): "treats some kids better than others" (reversed),
"makes math interesting", and "makes math easy to understand" (α = 0.73 and 0.74). Outcome: bachelor's degree or
higher by June 2021, from postsecondary administrative records. Covariates: grade-9 math score, socioeconomic status,
family structure, sex, race/ethnicity, school locale.

**Analysis.** Separate survey-weighted linear probability models for each grade on the same 14,259 students; the
difference θ11 − θ9 computed within each of 200 balanced-repeated-replication weights; m = 20; equivalence bound ±3
percentage points per SD.

**Results.** Grade 9: −0.2 points per SD (−1.3 to 0.9). Grade 11: +1.1 (−0.2 to 2.4). Difference +1.3 (−0.4 to 3.1),
equivalent within ±3 points. Grade 11 was at or above grade 9 in every sensitivity analysis. An exploratory grade-9 item,
"there is a teacher or other adult in school I can talk to about problems", was associated with +2.0 points per SD
(0.8 to 3.3).

## 5. Study 3: middle childhood vs early adolescence (NLSY79 Children and Young Adults)

**Data and measures.** The NLSY79 Children and Young Adults study follows the children of a 1979 national sample of
young women. Every two years, mothers answered identically worded items in the HOME section for ages 6–9 and the
section for ages 10–14: "How many times in the past week have you praised [child] for doing something worthwhile?"
(1988–2016; primary) and "How close does [child] feel toward you?" (1994–2016; secondary). Praise was capped at the 1988
top-code and normal-scored within survey year and section. Window scores averaged each child's reports. Outcome:
bachelor's degree or higher at the last young-adult interview, observed if that interview was at age 25 or older.
Covariates: sex, race/ethnicity, birth-year group, birth order, mother's age at birth, mother's education and AFQT
percentile, and childhood poverty share and mean log family income from the mother's interviews.

**Analysis.** Linear probability model with both windows; cluster-robust variance by mother; m = 20; equivalence bound
±3 points per SD.

**Results.** For 6,190 children of 3,138 mothers, praise at ages 6–9 was not associated with bachelor's attainment
(−0.1 points per SD; −1.5 to 1.2), while praise at 10–14 was (+1.6; 0.3 to 2.9). The difference (−1.7; −3.9 to 0.6) was
not statistically significant and equivalence was inconclusive. The same ordering held in every sensitivity analysis and
for closeness (6–9: +0.8; 10–14: +2.4). In an exploratory life-course comparison, "early adolescence only" and
"accumulation" models fit about equally well, and better than "middle childhood only".

## 6. Study 4 (exploratory): accumulation vs timing, NLSY97 1997–2000

Among 2,566 youths aged 12–14 in 1996 with mother-support reports in all four annual rounds and complete covariates,
each additional year described as very supportive was associated with 0.22 more grades completed (0.14 to 0.31). A
design-based test that all years carry equal weight did not reject accumulation (F(3, 115) = 0.83, p = .48). Among
restricted life-course models [@mishra2009; @smith2015], accumulation had the lowest AIC, though an unrestricted model
fit better still.

## 7. General discussion

Four analyses, three cohorts, two kinds of adults (parents and teachers), several measures and ages 6–17 give a
consistent answer. Supportive relationships are modestly associated with later educational attainment, and **no
analysis found reliable evidence that support measured earlier carries more weight than support measured later**. The
one estimate in that direction (fathers, age 12 vs 16) was statistically equivalent to no difference. Elsewhere the point
estimates favoured the later measurement: early adolescence over middle childhood, grade 11 over grade 9, and age 16
over 12 for mothers. For
fathers in NLSY97 and for teachers in HSLS:09, the data rule out differences larger than the pre-set bounds.

Three readings are compatible with this pattern. First, support may matter throughout development and accumulate,
which the NLSY97 four-year analysis directly supports. Second, support measured later may be closer in time to
educational decisions. Third, later reports may better capture stable, long-lasting relationships. None of these fits a
narrow early window. The results agree with the caution that adolescent sensitive periods are more asserted than
demonstrated [@fuhrmann2015], and with mentoring evidence that relationship duration matters [@grossman2002].

### 7.1 Limitations

The associations are observational. Youths who are easier, more capable or more engaged may both elicit and report
more support, and in Study 1 adjusting for a test score reduced the association. Measures differ across studies and are
single-informant. In single cohorts, age is entangled with birth year, household composition and distance to the
outcome. HSLS:09 grade comparisons involve different teachers. The CYA over-represents children of younger mothers among
those with adult outcomes. The plans were frozen privately in a public repository rather than registered.

### 7.2 Implications for youth mentoring

These results cannot show that mentoring works or when to deliver it. They do weaken the case for concentrating support at
one early age, including the freshman year, on the grounds that it will pay off more there. They are consistent with
designing for durable relationships that continue across transitions, and with offering support whenever young people can
be reached. Tests of timing for mentors will require designs that vary when relationships begin, ideally randomised.
