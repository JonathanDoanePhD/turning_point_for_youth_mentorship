# Supportive adults and educational attainment across development: a critical literature review

The Turning Point Project. Literature review, version 0.1, prepared 2026-11-12.

## How to read this review

- **Scope.** Every work cited here was checked against Crossref on 2026-11-12. The record for each one is in `citation_verification_log.jsonl`, `references.bib` and `evidence_matrix.csv`. Works that could not be checked are listed in `references_unverified.md` and are not cited as evidence below.
- **Depth of reading.** This is an abstract-level review. Findings come only from the abstracts we read. Abstracts came from Crossref or PubMed unless the matrix says otherwise. No full texts were read. Where the matrix says `metadata_only`, the work is cited for the concept it is known for, and no specific findings are attributed to it.
- **Numbers.** Effect sizes quoted below come from abstracts and are marked *(abstract)*. Any number recalled from memory is marked *(recalled; verify against full text)*. Before any figure here is reused in a funder report, grant application or public communication, it must be checked against the primary source.
- **Causal language.** Each section says whether its evidence is **causal** (randomized or credible quasi-experimental) or **correlational** (observational).

## 1. Developmental turning points, life-course theory, and sensitive periods

### Life-course theory and turning points

Life-course theory grew out of following the early child-development cohorts into mid-life. The main lessons were that lives are shaped by changing historical context, and that ideas about development must apply across the whole life span (Elder, 1998). Elder's broader principles of timing, linked lives and human agency (Elder, 1994) are cited here at the metadata level only. Clausen (1991) is cited, also at the metadata level, for the idea that adolescent "planful competence" shapes the adult life course *(recalled; verify against full text)*.

Rutter's (1996) critique matters most for this project. He concluded that "turning point" effects:

- are a heterogeneous range of lasting changes;
- usually involve particular qualities of experience as they affect *subsegments of the population*, rather than universal life-phase transitions;
- may *accentuate* existing characteristics rather than change them;
- have no single mechanism that could be tested as one hypothesis.

Laub and Sampson (1993) separated self-selection from cumulative processes. Using qualitative life histories from the Glueck men, they argued that both gradual and abrupt change in adulthood follow changes in social bonds. Sampson and Laub (2005) developed this into an age-graded theory of informal social control. In that theory, turning points are time-varying life events, and development combines person-environment interaction, random "developmental noise" and agency.

**Implication.** The foundational turning-point literature does *not* predict a single universal window such as freshman year. It predicts change that is specific to transitions and to individuals, alongside cumulative processes.

### Sensitive and critical periods

Knudsen (2004) gives the standard definitions:

- A **sensitive period** is a limited window in which experience has an especially strong effect.
- A **critical period** is a sensitive period in which the experience is essential and its effect is permanent.

Knudsen also stresses that a lot of plasticity remains after sensitive periods close. Thomas and Johnson (2008) concluded that there are several kinds of sensitive period, with different mechanisms, and that many may simply follow from ordinary postnatal brain development.

Several reviews make the case for adolescence:

- Steinberg (2005) described adolescence as a critical or sensitive period for reorganizing regulatory systems.
- Blakemore and Mills (2014) described the reorganization of the social brain in the second decade as *possibly* a sensitive period for adapting to the social environment.
- Dahl et al. (2018) argued that lives can "pivot rapidly" in adolescence and that there are windows of opportunity for policy.

**Caution.** Fuhrmann et al. (2015) wrote that although neuroplasticity data are *consistent with* adolescent sensitive periods, "concrete evidence for sensitive periods in adolescence is mostly lacking" *(abstract)*. They said firm evidence needs experiments that manipulate environmental input and compare children, adolescents and adults. Gabard-Durnam and McLaughlin (2020) likewise noted that knowledge of human sensitive periods is limited in many domains and comes mostly from methods developed in animals. Frankenhuis and Walasek (2020) review evolutionary models of when sensitive periods should arise, which gives a theory of *why* windows might exist.

**Observational data cannot settle this.** Age at exposure is confounded with:

- how long the exposure lasts and how much of it accumulates;
- selection into exposure, which itself changes with age;
- how close the exposure is to the outcome being measured.

Section 10 covers methods that partly address these problems.

## 2. Positive youth development and resilience

The positive youth development framework treats adolescents' strengths and the resources around them, including caring adults, as developmental assets. In Wave 1 of the 4-H Study, Lerner et al. (2005) found structural support for the "Five Cs" (competence, confidence, connection, character and caring), which loaded on a second-order PYD factor. Both PYD and taking part in youth programs were independently related to youth "contribution." These data are cross-sectional, from fifth graders. Scales et al. (2000) is cited for the developmental-assets framework at the metadata level only.

Masten (2001) concluded that resilience is common and comes from normal adaptive systems ("ordinary magic"). The biggest threats are those that damage protective systems, and relationships with competent, caring adults are a central example of a protective system *(recalled; verify against full text)*. Luthar et al. (2000) addressed criticisms of the resilience construct, including vague definitions, heterogeneity and *instability over time*, and warned about methodological pitfalls. Werner (1993), drawing on the Kauai cohort followed from birth to age 32, identified clusters of protective factors that let most high-risk children become competent adults.

**Timing relevance.** This literature mostly assumes protective relationships matter throughout development. Luthar et al.'s point about instability suggests that being protected at one age does not guarantee protection at another, which fits a transition-specific or individualized model better than a fixed window. The evidence is mainly correlational.

## 3. Formal mentoring programs

### Overall effectiveness (meta-analyses)

| Source | Studies | What the abstract says *(abstract)* |
|---|---|---|
| DuBois, Holloway et al. (2002) | 55 evaluations | Only a "modest or small" benefit for the average youth. Larger effects with more best practices and strong relationships. Youth facing environmental risk benefited most. Poorly implemented programs may harm youth with personal vulnerabilities. |
| DuBois, Portillo et al. (2011) | Not read | Only a partial abstract was available. It describes a meta-analysis of typical effectiveness and of the conditions for optimal outcomes, plus a developmental model of mentoring. No effect sizes or moderator results from this report are given here. **The age-moderation results must be pulled from the full text.** |
| Raposa et al. (2019) | 70 studies; 25,286 youth; mean age 12 | Significant overall effect, "within the medium/moderate range" relative to universal-prevention benchmarks. Larger effects with more male youth, more male mentors or mentors from helping professions, shorter meetings, and self-report measures. Authors urge realism about "the modest impact" of programs as currently run. The abstract does not report age as a significant moderator. |
| Christensen et al. (2020) | 48 studies; mean age 12.25 | Targeted, skills-based programs had an overall effect more than double that of non-specific relational programs. |
| van Dam et al. (2021) | 14 studies of youth-initiated mentoring | g = 0.30 overall and g = 0.40 when controlling for possible selection bias. Moderated by gender and publication year. |

### Randomized trials

- **Big Brothers Big Sisters community-based mentoring** (Grossman & Tierney, 1998; ages 10–16, 18-month follow-up). Mentored youth were less likely to start using drugs or alcohol, to hit someone, or to skip school. They felt more confident about school and got along better with their families *(abstract)*. This is causal but short-term, and it did not measure attainment.
- **BBBS school-based mentoring** (Herrera et al., 2011; N = 1,139, ages 9–16). Academic gains after the first year were *not sustained* into the second year *(abstract)*. A secondary analysis (Bayer et al., 2015) used two-stage least squares and suggests emotional closeness explains the academic effects. Closeness was not randomized, so this rests on instrumental-variable assumptions.
- **Becoming a Man** (Heller et al., 2017), a cognitive-behavioral program for disadvantaged Chicago youth. It reduced arrests and, in the first study, raised graduation rates by 12–19% *(abstract)*. The authors found "no positive evidence" that the effects worked through "a generic mentoring effect." This matters for the project: an adolescent program delivered through relationships appears to work through *cognitive* mechanisms, not through the relationship itself.
- **Check & Connect-type engagement monitoring** (Sinclair et al., 1998; N = 94 students with disabilities). Students who kept receiving the intervention through grade 9 were more engaged on two of three measures than students whose support ended after grade 8 *(abstract)*. This is the closest randomized evidence found for *extending* support across the high-school transition. The sample is small, and the outcome is engagement, not attainment.

### Duration, termination, and the developmental model

Grossman and Rhodes (2002) re-analyzed the BBBS trial sample. Matches lasting a year or more showed the most benefit, and very early terminations were linked to *declines* in functioning. **Older adolescents were more likely to be in matches that ended early** *(abstract)*. Duration was not randomized.

Rhodes et al. (2006) and Rhodes and DuBois (2008) set out a process model in which close, lasting bonds are the key mechanism. Christensen et al. (2020) challenges that model: purely relational approaches do less well than targeted ones.

Schwartz et al. (2013) studied youth-initiated mentoring among 16–18-year-olds who had dropped out. Longer-lasting relationships were associated with keeping educational gains at 3 years, and relationships lasted longer when youth chose their own mentors. This finding is observational.

**Timing relevance.** Program evaluations mostly cover early adolescence (mean age about 12). That narrow age range makes it hard to test whether age changes effects. Age appears in the abstracts read only as a predictor of early termination, and the Raposa et al. (2019) abstract lists no age moderation. Whether age was tested and came out null is a question for the full text.

## 4. Natural (informal) mentoring

Almost all natural-mentoring evidence is **correlational**, and much of it comes from the Add Health study.

- **Van Dam et al. (2018), meta-analysis of 30 studies.** Having a natural mentor was associated with youth outcomes at r = .106, and the quality of the relationship at r = .208. The largest associations were with social-emotional and academic/vocational outcomes. Risk status did not moderate the association *(abstract)*.
- **DuBois and Silverthorn (2005), Add Health Wave III.** Having a mentor was associated with finishing high school and attending college, with prior functioning controlled. However, the effects of risk exposure were generally larger than the protective association with mentoring.
- **Erickson et al. (2009).** Found a "powerful net influence" of mentors on educational success. Better-resourced youth were more likely to *have* mentors, but less-resourced youth *benefited more*, especially from teacher mentors. In other words, mentors work as both a compensatory and a complementary resource.
- **Raposa et al. (2018).** Low-income adolescents had less access to natural mentors. Their mentors were more often family or friends who gave practical support, rather than role models or career advisors.
- **Hagler and Rhodes (2018), counterfactual analysis.** Adults aged 33–42 who had a natural mentor in adolescence or emerging adulthood had higher educational attainment. "Weak-tie" mentors (teachers, coaches, employers) were associated with higher attainment and income. "Strong-tie" mentors were associated with more close friends and lower income.
- **Hurd et al. (2012)**, with at-risk African American youth (mean age 17.8). Natural mentors were linked to attainment through racial private regard and beliefs about the value of school.
- **Fruiht and Chan (2018).** Adolescent mentorship moderated the link between parents' college attendance and the young person's own attainment.
- **Timpe and Lunkenheimer (2015).** Found no overall earnings association, but did find one for fatherless youth who had male mentors.
- **Hurd and Zimmerman (2010).** Tracked health trajectories in emerging adulthood.

### Timing of mentoring: what exists (the core gap)

We searched specifically for studies of the *age at which* a supportive relationship began. Only one directly relevant study was found:

- **Fruiht and Wray-Lake (2013)**, using Add Health Waves III–IV (N = 2,409, ages 18–27), classified *when the mentor became important*: before, during or after high school. Findings *(abstract)*:
  - Teacher-mentors were more predictive of attainment than other mentor types.
  - **Having a mentor *after* high school predicted the most attainment.**
  - Kin mentors appeared more important *during* high school, and community mentors *before* high school.
  - Results were consistent across ethnic groups.

  The authors suggest "different types of mentors may be particularly useful at specific points in development."

  **Caveats.** Timing was reported retrospectively. The post-high-school association is almost certainly confounded, because people who stay in education have more chances to meet mentors there, so the outcome partly causes the exposure. Even so, it is the most direct evidence against a single adolescent window, and it points toward *mentor-type-by-timing* interactions.

- **Miranda-Chan et al. (2016)** state in their abstract that adolescence is "a time during which positive adults outside the familial context may be most influential." This is a premise, not something the study tested. Their data link adolescent community mentors to adult attainment.

- **Hagler and Rhodes (2018)** combined adolescence and emerging adulthood into one exposure, so their study cannot separate timing.

No randomized or quasi-experimental study was found that varied the *age at onset* of a mentoring relationship.

## 5. Teacher-student relationships, engagement, belonging, and school transitions

### Teacher relationships

Roorda et al. (2011) meta-analyzed 99 studies from preschool through high school. Associations with engagement were medium to large, and associations with achievement were small to medium *(abstract)*. Crucially for timing:

> "Overall, stronger effects were found in the higher grades. Nevertheless, the effects of negative relationships were stronger in primary than in secondary school." *(abstract)*

This is the clearest *quantitative* evidence of age-graded associations in this review. It supports a stronger role for positive teacher relationships in adolescence, but it is correlational and between-study, so grade is confounded with measures, designs and samples. Roorda et al.'s (2017) meta-analytic update is cited at the metadata level only.

Hamre and Pianta (2001) found that kindergarten teacher-child relational negativity predicted outcomes through grade 8, especially for boys and children with behavior problems. Early relationships therefore have long reach, which fits an accumulation or "chains of risk" model.

Wang and Eccles (2012) followed students from grades 7 to 11. Several dimensions of engagement declined, and support from teachers, peers and parents predicted *different* dimensions of engagement.

Hill and Tyson (2009) meta-analyzed 50 studies of parental involvement in middle school. "Academic socialization" had the strongest association with achievement, and the authors argue it suits the developmental stage of early adolescence *(abstract)*. This is a developmental-fit argument, not a window argument.

### Engagement and belonging

Fredricks et al. (2004) describe engagement as multidimensional and presumed to be malleable. Goodenow (1993) and Allen et al. (2018) are cited at the metadata level for school belonging measurement and meta-analysis. No findings are attributed to them here.

### Stage-environment fit and the high-school (freshman) transition

Eccles et al. (1993) argued that declines in early adolescence partly reflect a *mismatch* between adolescents' needs and what school and home environments offer. Their examples centered on the move to junior high or middle school.

On the transition into high school:

- Benner and Graham (2009) followed 1,979 urban students from grades 7 to 10. Students who were doing well before the transition showed *disruptions* in psychological functioning and grades when they entered high school, and many kept struggling. The disruption was larger for African American and Latino students when the share of their own ethnic group fell *(abstract)*.
- Benner (2011) noted that the high-school transition has received less research attention than earlier transitions.
- Neild et al. (2008) used Philadelphia records and found that ninth-grade outcomes "are not simply proxies" for students' earlier characteristics and "add substantially" to predicting dropout *(abstract)*.

The widely cited Chicago "on-track" work (Allensworth & Easton) is gray literature. It could not be verified in Crossref and is listed as unverified.

## 6. Self-efficacy, identity, and expectations

Bandura (1977, 1982) is cited for self-efficacy theory, and Marcia (1966) for identity status, both at the metadata level. Erikson's identity theory is a book and is in the unverified list.

Oyserman et al. (2006) randomized low-income eighth graders (141 treatment, 123 control) to a brief school-based intervention. It linked academic "possible selves" to concrete strategies. The intervention improved grades, test scores and academic initiative and reduced absences and misbehavior over 2 years, with effects mediated by changes in possible selves *(abstract)*. **This is causal evidence that identity-focused support can work in the year before the high-school transition.** It does not show that the same intervention would work less well at other ages.

Mello (2008), using NELS:88 data from ages 14 to 26, found that adolescent educational expectations predicted adult attainment, with differences by gender. This is correlational.

## 7. Economic mobility and the timing of interventions

These studies give the most credible evidence on *age-varying* effects, but the exposure they study is neighborhoods, not relationships.

- **Chetty, Hendren and Katz (2016), Moving to Opportunity (randomized).** Moving to a lower-poverty area *before age 13* raised college attendance and earnings. Moving as an *adolescent* had "slightly negative impacts, perhaps because of disruption effects." The authors interpret the decline with age at move as showing that the duration of exposure matters *(abstract)*.
- **Chetty and Hendren (2018), quasi-experimental study of more than 7 million families who moved.** Children's outcomes improve *linearly* with years of exposure, at about 4% per year *(abstract of NBER w23001 working-paper version; verify against the QJE version)*. Linear dose-response is evidence for **accumulation**, not a sensitive period. The MTO finding that adolescent moves can harm shows that a *disruptive transition* in adolescence can cancel out gains from a better environment.
- **Cunha and Heckman (2007)** model skill formation with self-productivity and **dynamic complementarity**: earlier skills raise the productivity of later investment. Heckman (2006) summarizes this as "early inputs strongly affect the productivity of later inputs."
- **Cunha, Heckman and Schennach (2010)** add an important detail. Substitutability between investment periods *decreases* in later stages for **cognitive** skills but *increases* in later stages for **noncognitive** skills, and "for some configurations of disadvantage and outcomes, it is optimal to invest relatively more in the later stages of childhood" *(abstract)*. Adult relationships plausibly act on noncognitive skills, so this framework predicts *more* room for adolescent remediation in that domain.
- **Chetty et al. (2022)** found that area-level economic connectedness (cross-SES friendship) is among the strongest predictors of upward mobility. This is ecological and associational, with no age dimension.

## 8. Heterogeneity of intervention effects by age

- **Mentoring meta-analyses.** No age moderation is reported in the abstracts of DuBois et al. (2002), Raposa et al. (2019), Christensen et al. (2020) or van Dam et al. (2018, 2021). Whether age was tested and found null, or not tested, must be checked in the full texts, especially DuBois et al. (2011). We are not stating any recalled age-moderation result here.
- **Yeager, Dahl and Dweck (2018)** propose that traditional school-based preventive interventions work reasonably well for children but less well for *middle adolescents*. Interventions that respect adolescents' desire for status and respect may succeed instead. This is a hypothesis about *intervention-by-age fit*, not about a sensitive period for relationships.
- **Yeager et al. (2019), National Study of Learning Mindsets.** A growth-mindset intervention lasting under an hour improved grades among lower-achieving students and raised advanced-math course taking in a nationally representative US secondary-school sample. Effects held up where peer norms supported the message *(abstract)*. The sample was ninth graders *(recalled; verify against full text)*. The study shows that the transition year is a *feasible and responsive* moment. It cannot show the year is *uniquely* responsive, because no other grade was randomized.
- **Yeager and Walton (2011)** explain how brief interventions can have lasting effects through recursive processes in school settings, which is a mechanism by which an early-transition intervention could compound over time.
- **Roorda et al. (2011)** report grade-level differences (section 5).
- **Grossman and Rhodes (2002)** report higher early termination among older adolescents (section 3).

## 9. Family structure and single-parent households

McLanahan et al. (2013) reviewed designs that try to identify the causal effect of father absence: lagged-dependent-variable, growth-curve, individual and sibling fixed effects, natural experiments and propensity score matching. Their conclusion is that more rigorous designs "continue to find negative effects," but these are smaller than cross-sectional estimates. The evidence is strongest for high school graduation, social-emotional adjustment and adult mental health *(abstract)*. Amato (2005) reviews family formation change. Its abstract is descriptive only.

For this project, family structure is both a likely confounder and a possible effect modifier. Timpe and Lunkenheimer (2015) found earnings associations with male mentors only among fatherless youth, and Erickson et al. (2009) found compensatory benefits for less-resourced youth. Whether parental separation *at a particular age* matters more is not covered in the abstracts read. Selection into family structure remains a serious threat.

## 10. Methods relevant to the design

- **Reporting.** STROBE gives a 22-item checklist for observational studies (von Elm et al., 2007).
- **Time-varying confounding.** When earlier support affects later confounders, such as grades that in turn shape access to mentors, standard adjustment is biased. Marginal structural models fitted with inverse-probability-of-treatment weighting address this (Robins et al., 2000). Target-trial emulation (Hernán & Robins, 2016) gives a framework for stating, for example, "start a mentor relationship in grade 9 vs. grade 11" as an explicit hypothetical trial.
- **Unmeasured confounding.** E-values report how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be to explain away an association (VanderWeele & Ding, 2017).
- **Missing data.** Multiple imputation by chained equations (White et al., 2011), building on Rubin (1987).
- **Panel models.** The classic cross-lagged panel model mixes stable between-person differences with within-person effects. The random-intercept cross-lagged panel model separates them (Hamaker et al., 2015). This matters if the project tests whether *changes* in support come before *changes* in engagement.
- **Life-course model selection (most relevant to H1–H7).**
  - Ben-Shlomo and Kuh (2002) and Kuh et al. (2003) set out critical-period, accumulation and chains-of-risk models. These are cited at the metadata level and as a glossary respectively.
  - Mishra et al. (2009) recommend comparing *nested* models for accumulation, critical-period and effect-modification hypotheses against a saturated model. They warn that analyzing only a cumulative score can mislead *(abstract)*.
  - Smith et al. (2015) built a LASSO-based selection method. It works well in moderately sized samples but less well for change hypotheses or **highly correlated exposures** *(abstract)*. Relationship support measured repeatedly will be highly correlated, so this limitation applies directly.
  - Smith et al. (2016) extended the method to continuous exposures, confounding and missing data.
  - Zhu et al. (2021) and Smith, Smith and Dunn (2021) describe the structured life-course modeling approach (SLCMA) with selective inference for valid post-selection confidence intervals.
  - **Model selection is not causal identification.** These tools say which timing pattern best *describes* the data, conditional on measured confounding.

## What the evidence does and does not establish about developmental timing

**The motivating belief:** that freshman year (grade 9) is an especially consequential window for supportive adult relationships.

### What supports giving the transition special attention

1. **Ninth-grade performance predicts dropout.** It adds predictive information beyond prior characteristics (Neild et al., 2008). This is **predictive, not causal**.
2. **The high-school transition is often disruptive.** Students show disruptions in functioning and grades, especially when the school's ethnic composition shifts (Benner & Graham, 2009). This is **correlational**.
3. **Stage-environment mismatch at school transitions is theoretically grounded** (Eccles et al., 1993). Engagement declines from grades 7 to 11 (Wang & Eccles, 2012).
4. **Brief interventions delivered around the transition can be effective.** This includes growth mindset in secondary school (Yeager et al., 2019), possible-selves in grade 8 (Oyserman et al., 2006), and extending engagement monitoring through grade 9 (Sinclair et al., 1998). All three are **causal**.
5. **Teacher-relationship associations are, on average, stronger in higher grades** (Roorda et al., 2011). This is **correlational and between-study**.

### What the evidence does not establish

1. **No study found compares the effect of the *same* relationship-based support started in grade 9 against other grades.** Without that comparison, "consequential" cannot be told apart from "convenient and measurable."
2. **The strongest age-varying causal evidence favors accumulation or exposure, not an adolescent window.** Neighborhood effects scale linearly with years of exposure (Chetty & Hendren, 2018). In MTO, moving in adolescence produced no gain or slight harm (Chetty et al., 2016). By analogy, this suggests that *disrupting* relationships at adolescent transitions may be costly, and that *duration* of support matters. The analogy is from neighborhoods to relationships and is untested.
3. **Adolescent sensitive periods are mostly hypothesized in neuroscience,** and "concrete evidence ... is mostly lacking" (Fuhrmann et al., 2015).
4. **The only mentor-timing study found** links *post*-high-school mentors to the most attainment, with mentor type interacting with timing (Fruiht & Wray-Lake, 2013). This argues against a single grade-9 window, although confounding is serious.
5. **Duration and match stability matter in mentoring programs,** and older adolescents' matches end early more often (Grossman & Rhodes, 2002). Age, duration and match risk are tangled together.
6. **Economic models of skill formation imply complementarity across stages** (Cunha & Heckman, 2007). For noncognitive skills, later investment can partly substitute for earlier investment (Cunha et al., 2010). This fits a **cumulative or complementary** model better than a closed window.
7. **Turning-point theory expects effects specific to transitions and to individuals,** often concentrated in population subgroups rather than at universal ages (Rutter, 1996; Sampson & Laub, 2005).

### Bottom line

The literature reviewed supports treating the ninth-grade transition as a **high-risk, high-leverage *transition*** where supports are feasible and some brief interventions work. It does **not** support claiming that relationships in freshman year have a *larger causal effect on attainment* than equivalent relationships at other ages.

The more defensible working hypotheses combine three ideas:

- accumulation or duration of support;
- transition-specific vulnerability, where losing support at a transition is costly;
- heterogeneity by person, mentor type and context.

## Unresolved questions / gaps this project could address

1. **Age at onset vs. duration vs. recency.** No study found separates these three for supportive adult relationships. SLCMA-type model comparison (Mishra et al., 2009; Smith et al., 2015, 2016) applied to repeated measures of support could at least show which *descriptive* timing model fits best. Pre-specified sensitivity analyses could test robustness to correlated exposures.
2. **Transition-anchored vs. age-anchored timing.** Studies rarely ask whether effects follow *chronological age* or *school transitions* (grade 6/7, grade 9, the move to post-secondary). Districts with different grade configurations, such as K-8 vs. middle-school systems, could provide natural variation.
3. **Age moderation in mentoring meta-analyses.** Abstracts do not report it. Full-text extraction from DuBois et al. (2011) and Raposa et al. (2019) is needed to say whether age was tested and was null, or simply not tested. Program samples cluster around age 12, which limits any such test.
4. **Mentor type × timing.** Fruiht and Wray-Lake (2013) and Hagler and Rhodes (2018) suggest teacher and weak-tie mentors matter most for attainment. Whether this varies by stage is untested causally.
5. **Loss or disruption of support at transitions.** The MTO adolescent result and the harm from early termination suggest *discontinuity* may matter as much as onset. Few studies model losing support as an exposure.
6. **Selection and reverse causation in natural mentoring.** Youth with more resources form more mentoring ties (Erickson et al., 2009; Raposa et al., 2018). Target-trial emulation with time-varying confounding methods (Hernán & Robins, 2016; Robins et al., 2000) and E-values would strengthen inference.
7. **Long-run attainment in randomized trials.** Most mentoring RCTs follow youth for 18 months or less, and the school-based mentoring gains faded (Herrera et al., 2011). Long-term linkage of past RCT samples to attainment records is a clear opportunity.
8. **Individualized timing.** Rutter (1996) and Luthar et al. (2000) imply that the window may differ by person. Approaches that model heterogeneous effects, such as subgroup moderation set out in advance, would test this better than population averages.

## Search methods and limitations

- **Date of searches:** 2026-11-12.
- **Sources used:**
  - Crossref REST API for all metadata verification, by DOI lookup and `query.bibliographic`.
  - PubMed E-utilities (esearch/efetch by DOI) and Europe PMC for abstracts missing from Crossref.
  - Semantic Scholar Graph API for a few abstracts (DuBois et al., 2011; Cunha & Heckman, 2007; Gabard-Durnam & McLaughlin, 2020). This API was rate-limited.
  - The NBER working-paper page for the Chetty and Hendren (2018) abstract.
  - OpenAlex was not used because it rate-limits this environment.
- **Candidate selection:** Started from a pre-specified list of foundational works, meta-analyses, systematic reviews and RCTs across the ten topics, plus targeted Crossref searches for studies on the *timing* of mentoring and support. The timing searches returned mostly irrelevant results. The timing-specific studies included here came from the targeted list.
- **Verification rule:** A work was included only when Crossref returned a record whose title matched the expected title (similarity of at least 0.75 after normalization, plus a manual check of authors and venue). Several remembered DOIs were wrong and were replaced with the Crossref-returned DOI after checking authors and venue. The log records the title-similarity score.
- **Depth:** Abstract-level. 75 of 87 works were read at abstract level, and 12 at metadata level only. **No full texts were read.** Effect sizes and moderator results may be incomplete or lack context.
- **Not a systematic review:** This review did not follow PRISMA. There was no registered protocol, no double screening, no risk-of-bias assessment and no exhaustive search of PsycINFO, ERIC, Web of Science or Scopus. Publication bias and selective inclusion are possible. In particular, the search may have missed other studies on the age at which mentoring began.
- **Gray literature excluded:** Chicago Consortium on-track reports and the MDRC and P/PV program reports were left out of the main evidence base. See `references_unverified.md`.
- **Organizational note:** Statistics quoted here must be checked against primary sources before use in any external, funder or public material. Nothing here concerns individual participants.


## Addendum (2026-11-12): full-text check of age moderation

The review above was written from abstracts. Open full texts were then read for 10 works (details, verbatim quotes
and locations: `fulltext_age_moderation.md` and `.csv`). Main points:

- No meta-analysis that tested age found significant moderation of mentoring effects (`dubois2002`, `raposa2019`,
  `vandam2018`, `vandam2021`). Non-significant tilts point in different directions.
- Programme samples cover mean ages of roughly 9–16, and natural-mentor samples 13–24. No full text compares early
  childhood with adolescence.
- Relationship length did not predict effects across programmes (`dubois2002`, `raposa2019`). Within one school-based
  trial, longer matches went with larger gains, but the authors note this may reflect selection (`herrera2011`, public
  trial report).

- **Update 2026-11-13:** the PI supplied full texts of `dubois2011`, `grossman2002` and `rhodes2005`. DuBois et al. (2011) coded age as
  <8, 8–10, 11–14 and 15–18 and found age unrelated to effect size, with favorable effects in every age group. Grossman and Rhodes
  (2002) found that age predicts how long matches last (13–16 year olds' matches ended sooner) and that benefits concentrate in
  matches of a year or more. Rhodes et al. (2005) found youth age unrelated to relationship quality, while longer matches had
  higher quality. Details: `fulltext_age_moderation.md`.
